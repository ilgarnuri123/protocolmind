TITLE: OFFICIAL LETTERS AND FORMAL CORRESPONDENCE

SECTION 1. PURPOSE

Official correspondence in protocol and diplomatic settings must be:
- respectful
- accurate
- restrained
- institutionally appropriate
- clear in purpose
- correct in title usage

The letter should reflect office, hierarchy, and context.

SECTION 2. MAIN DOCUMENT TYPES

Common protocol-related written documents include:
- invitation letter
- thank-you letter
- congratulatory letter
- condolence letter
- note verbale
- briefing note
- talking points
- meeting scenario
- protocol note
- visit program cover letter

SECTION 3. GENERAL STYLE PRINCIPLES

Official letters should generally:
- use formal address
- avoid emotional exaggeration
- remain concise
- state purpose clearly
- reflect institutional dignity
- avoid slang, excessive warmth, or vague wording
- maintain correct names, titles, and dates

SECTION 4. INVITATION LETTER

An invitation letter should usually contain:
- full and correct title of sender
- full and correct title of recipient
- institutional basis for invitation
- purpose of visit or meeting
- proposed dates if known
- polite invitation formula
- closing formula reflecting respect

Typical structure:
1. formal salutation
2. statement of pleasure or honour
3. invitation purpose
4. date or time frame
5. expression of hope for acceptance
6. closing

SECTION 5. THANK-YOU LETTER

A thank-you letter should:
- refer clearly to the meeting, visit, reception, or hospitality
- express appreciation in formal tone
- mention the value of the exchange where relevant
- reinforce good institutional relations
- close courteously

It should not be too long.

SECTION 6. NOTE VERBALE

A note verbale is a formal diplomatic communication usually written in the third person and without personal signature style.

Typical features:
- institutional sender
- no personal salutation like “Dear Mr.”
- formal diplomatic formula
- concise statement of purpose
- expression of compliments at the beginning and end

ProtocolMind should preserve correct note verbale style when requested.

SECTION 7. BRIEFING NOTE

A briefing note should be structured and practical.

Typical sections:
- subject
- purpose
- background
- key facts
- participants
- protocol sensitivities
- recommended line or position

A briefing note is not a letter.
It is an internal decision-support document.

SECTION 8. FORM OF ADDRESS

At first reference, use the full official title.

Examples:
- His Excellency Mr. ...
- Honorable Speaker ...
- Madam Chairperson ...
- Minister ...
- Ambassador ...

After first reference, shorter forms may be used if style allows.

SECTION 9. OPENING FORMULAS

Possible formal openings:
- I have the honour to...
- I am pleased to convey...
- On behalf of...
- It is my pleasure to extend...
- Please accept the assurances of my highest consideration.
- I would like to express my sincere appreciation...

The opening must match the format.
For example:
- invitation letters may be warmer
- note verbale remains more formulaic
- briefing notes are factual

SECTION 10. CLOSING FORMULAS

Formal closings may include:
- Please accept, Excellency, the assurances of my highest consideration.
- Please accept the expression of my highest esteem.
- Yours sincerely.
- Respectfully.
- With renewed appreciation.

The closure should fit the hierarchy and institution.

SECTION 11. TONE MANAGEMENT

Protocol writing should usually be:
- formal
- calm
- polished
- not theatrical
- not overly legal unless needed
- not casual

When writing for heads of institution, use stronger ceremonial respect.
When writing for working-level coordination, use cleaner operational formality.

SECTION 12. INVITATION CONTENT EXAMPLE LOGIC

An invitation letter for an official parliamentary visit may include:
- invitation from Speaker to Speaker
- purpose of strengthening interparliamentary relations
- proposed visit date
- expression of confidence that the visit will contribute to cooperation

SECTION 13. THANK-YOU CONTENT EXAMPLE LOGIC

A thank-you letter may include:
- appreciation for warm reception
- reference to fruitful discussions
- confidence in continued cooperation
- good wishes to the recipient and institution

SECTION 14. COMMON MISTAKES

Frequent mistakes include:
- wrong title
- misspelled name
- wrong institution
- too much emotion
- unclear purpose
- too many adjectives
- casual or chat-like wording
- incorrect date or place
- mixing note verbale style with personal letter style

SECTION 15. PROTOCOLMIND WRITING STANDARD

When ProtocolMind produces official letters, it should:
- identify correct document type
- use proper title and hierarchy
- write in formal, polished style
- keep structure clear
- avoid unnecessary verbosity
- produce text that can be used as a working draft immediately
