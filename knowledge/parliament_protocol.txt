TITLE: PARLIAMENTARY PROTOCOL

SECTION 1. DEFINITION

Parliamentary protocol is the body of formal rules, practices, traditions, and sequencing standards that govern official parliamentary meetings, visits, receptions, ceremonies, interparliamentary contacts, committee sessions, delegation exchanges, and institutional hospitality within a parliamentary environment.

It applies to:
- Speakers and Deputy Speakers
- parliamentary committees
- interparliamentary friendship groups
- foreign parliamentary delegations
- secretariats and international relations departments
- ceremonial visits and courtesy meetings
- parliamentary conferences and assemblies

Parliamentary protocol combines state protocol, institutional protocol, and diplomatic courtesy.

SECTION 2. CORE PRINCIPLE

In parliamentary protocol, dignity of the institution is central.

The protocol officer must protect:
- the authority of the Speaker
- the institutional prestige of Parliament
- the formal equality and dignity of the guest delegation
- the clarity of rank and position
- the orderly conduct of the event

The Speaker or Presiding Officer represents the institution, not merely an individual officeholder.

SECTION 3. MAIN PARLIAMENTARY ACTORS

Parliamentary protocol often involves:
- Speaker / Chairperson / President of Parliament
- Deputy Speakers
- Committee Chairs
- Heads of parliamentary delegations
- Members of Parliament
- Secretary General or Chief of Staff
- Heads of departments
- Parliamentary protocol and international relations staff
- Foreign parliamentary delegations
- Ambassadors and diplomatic corps when relevant

SECTION 4. PARLIAMENTARY PRECEDENCE

Within a parliamentary setting, protocol usually follows this logic:

1. Speaker / President / Chairperson of Parliament
2. Deputy Speakers
3. Chairs of major committees
4. Heads of official parliamentary delegations
5. Members of Parliament according to function and rank
6. Senior parliamentary officials
7. Advisers and technical staff

If a foreign parliamentary principal is visiting, that guest should receive visible parity and full dignity appropriate to their office.

For example:
- a foreign Speaker should be received by the host Speaker where possible
- a foreign committee chair may be received by the equivalent host committee chair or another suitably senior official
- protocol should avoid status mismatch unless unavoidable

SECTION 5. OFFICIAL PARLIAMENTARY VISITS

An official parliamentary visit may include:
- airport or entrance reception
- transfer to Parliament
- official greeting at entrance
- handshake and photo moment
- bilateral meeting between principals
- expanded meeting with delegations
- tour of parliamentary building if appropriate
- signing ceremony if applicable
- official lunch or dinner
- press statement or media appearance
- exchange of gifts
- farewell and departure

Every movement between these elements must be pre-planned.

SECTION 6. RECEPTION OF A FOREIGN SPEAKER

When receiving a foreign Speaker, the following should be prepared in advance:
- correct title and full style
- composition of guest delegation
- correct composition of host receiving line
- room assignment
- flag placement
- seating logic
- interpreters
- press and photo sequence
- greeting order
- gift exchange if applicable
- food restrictions and meal protocol if a meal is scheduled

The Speaker’s route through the building should be clean, direct, and controlled.

SECTION 7. ENTRANCE AND GREETING

At a parliamentary building, arrival should normally be received by:
- the appropriate protocol officer
- the relevant senior parliamentary official
- the host principal where planned
- supporting host staff positioned discreetly

Greeting sequence should be short and dignified.
No confusion should appear over:
- who stands where
- who greets first
- whether the delegation follows immediately
- where the photo moment happens

SECTION 8. MEETING FORMATS

Common parliamentary meeting formats include:

1. Courtesy call
A short formal meeting, usually symbolic, often with a handshake photo and brief exchange.

2. Bilateral meeting
Speaker with Speaker, or principal with principal, often with a small number of senior accompanying officials.

3. Expanded delegation meeting
Larger format involving members of both delegations, advisers, committee representatives, and interpreters.

4. Committee-level meeting
Committee Chairs, committee members, experts, and staff in structured discussion.

5. Interparliamentary assembly or conference format
Larger multilateral environment with podiums, plenary speaking order, flags, and complex seating.

SECTION 9. BILATERAL PARLIAMENTARY MEETING

In a bilateral meeting:
- the two principals should sit opposite one another at the center
- senior parliamentary officials should sit nearest to the principal
- interpreters should be placed in positions that support direct exchange
- delegation symmetry should be preserved where feasible

Documents required may include:
- meeting agenda
- talking points
- briefing notes
- biographical notes
- institutional overview
- protocol sequence sheet

SECTION 10. EXPANDED DELEGATION MEETING

For expanded meetings:
- seating must reflect hierarchy but also functional relevance
- committee chairs, heads of department, and senior advisers should be positioned logically
- overloading the table with too many people should be avoided
- a clean, symmetrical, and readable arrangement is preferable

Each seat should be confirmed before principals enter.

SECTION 11. COMMITTEE PROTOCOL

When parliamentary committees meet foreign delegations:
- equivalence of level is important
- the host committee chair should occupy the central host position
- if multiple committees are represented, their relative standing should be determined in advance
- technical experts should not visually outrank elected or principal figures

Committee meetings should still preserve parliamentary dignity even if discussion is highly technical.

SECTION 12. TITLES IN PARLIAMENTARY SETTINGS

Use titles carefully.
Examples may include:
- Speaker of the Milli Majlis
- Chairperson of the Committee on International Relations
- Head of Delegation
- Member of Parliament
- Deputy Speaker
- Secretary General

At first formal mention, use the full title.
Later, use the shorter accepted form.

Never downgrade a parliamentary figure by using an informal or incomplete title where a formal one is required.

SECTION 13. FORMS OF ADDRESS

Acceptable formal address depends on:
- national custom
- institutional style
- official language
- level of formality

Examples:
- Mr. Speaker
- Madam Speaker
- Mr. Chairman
- Madam Chairperson
- Honourable Member
- Distinguished Delegate

In written material, names and titles must be checked carefully.

SECTION 14. PARLIAMENTARY FLAGS AND SYMBOLS

In parliamentary protocol, visual setting may include:
- state flag
- parliamentary flag or institutional emblem
- guest state flag where appropriate
- flags of interparliamentary organizations where relevant

Flags and symbols must:
- be balanced
- be placed correctly
- reflect institutional dignity
- never appear improvised or uneven

SECTION 15. PHOTO PROTOCOL

Photo moments in parliamentary settings may include:
- arrival handshake
- entrance photo
- bilateral table photo
- standing photo with flags
- full delegation photo
- gift exchange photo
- signing photo

Every photo point should be pre-decided.
Protocol must ensure:
- correct standing order
- central placement of principals
- proper flag background
- no staff visible in the main frame unless intended

SECTION 16. GIFT EXCHANGE

If gifts are exchanged in parliamentary visits:
- the moment should be placed naturally in the program
- the presentation order should be clear
- gifts should be institutionally appropriate
- symbolic value is usually more important than material value
- the gift should be logistically easy to carry and present

SECTION 17. OFFICIAL MEALS IN PARLIAMENTARY PROTOCOL

An official parliamentary lunch or dinner should reflect:
- host rank
- guest rank
- bilateral parity
- speaking and toast logic
- dietary restrictions
- route and seating flow

If the Speaker hosts the meal, this should be visually and procedurally evident.

SECTION 18. DRESS CODE

Parliamentary protocol usually requires:
- dark business suit or equivalent formal attire for most official meetings
- national protocol variations where relevant
- more ceremonial dress only if clearly required by event type

Protocol should ensure all host staff understand the expected dress level.

SECTION 19. PRESS AND MEDIA

Media access in parliamentary settings should be controlled.
Important questions:
- Is the meeting open or closed?
- Is there a doorstep statement?
- Is there a press spray at the beginning?
- Is photography limited to the first minutes?
- Are journalists entering the chamber, hallway, or only photo zone?

Protocol and press teams must coordinate closely.

SECTION 20. SPEAKING ORDER

Speaking order should be determined in advance.

In many parliamentary contexts:
- host principal opens
- guest principal responds
- then delegation members speak as relevant

At conferences or multilateral events:
- speaking order may follow agreed alphabetical, institutional, regional, or chair-determined rules

SECTION 21. TOUR OF PARLIAMENT

If a parliamentary building tour is included:
- route should be short, safe, and meaningful
- historical or symbolic spaces may be highlighted
- movement should not appear chaotic
- photography permissions should be known
- security restrictions should already be coordinated

SECTION 22. CEREMONIAL ELEMENTS

Possible ceremonial elements include:
- signing guest book
- laying flowers or wreaths where appropriate
- exchange of commemorative items
- official group photo
- chamber visit
- protocol announcement or escort

Each such element must be briefed and sequenced.

SECTION 23. PREPARATION CHECKLIST FOR PARLIAMENTARY VISITS

Before the visit, confirm:
- titles and spellings
- delegation list
- arrival time
- route and transport
- receiving line
- room allocation
- seating chart
- flags and emblems
- nameplates
- interpreters
- briefing folders
- gift arrangements
- media arrangements
- official meal details
- farewell sequence

SECTION 24. COMMON MISTAKES IN PARLIAMENTARY PROTOCOL

Frequent mistakes:
- wrong title for the Speaker or committee chair
- unbalanced seating
- weak parity between host and guest delegation
- incorrect entrance route
- no clear photo point
- host not ready on time
- too many people at the table
- interpreters placed badly
- confusion about speaking order
- missing nameplates or incorrect spellings
- media entering at the wrong moment

SECTION 25. OUTPUT STANDARD FOR PROTOCOLMIND

When answering parliamentary protocol questions, ProtocolMind should:
- preserve parliamentary dignity
- reflect institutional hierarchy
- respect equivalence of parliamentary offices
- generate practical visit structures
- produce formal meeting notes, letters, and protocol sequences
- avoid casual administrative language
- support Speaker-level and committee-level scenarios clearly
